\chapter*{\myAbstractTitle}

In a federated identity infrastructure, a single identity provider authenticates users on behalf 
of many service providers and thereby becomes the trust anchor of the entire federation. 
A compromised or misconfigured provider does not affect one application but can undermine the 
security of every service relying on it. 
Despite this exposure, systematic security monitoring at the application layer of such providers 
remains largely absent, and existing approaches in this domain focus on operational availability 
rather than on security.

This thesis presents a monitoring system that observes a Shibboleth identity provider from within 
its own application layer. 
The system combines three sources that observe a login at different points, namely the audit log, 
the configuration and metadata of the service providers, and two interceptors inside the 
authentication flow. 
Records from these sources are pseudonymised at their origin, transmitted to a backend located 
outside the trust boundary of the provider, correlated with one another and evaluated against a 
catalogue of fifteen detection rules. 
The rules are derived from vulnerabilities and attack classes documented in a systematic survey 
of federated identity security and from the security recommendations of the OASIS SAML Security 
and Privacy Considerations. 

The evaluation examines how reliably the records can be correlated, whether the detection rules 
behave as specified, and what the resulting system costs. 
The correlation found a counterpart for all 7{,}236 reports. 
Where the audit log records the identifier of the request, each report is assigned unambiguously, 
independent of how many logins take place at the same time. 
Where the identifier is missing, a heuristic strategy compares the pseudonymised subject and 
address instead. 
In every tested scenario it assigned each report correctly, as long as one of these two values 
distinguishes the users. 
Only the fallback strategy based on time alone made wrong assignments, and only between logins at 
the same service provider that were less than 100\,ms apart. 
All fifteen rules were verified against a real search index and thirteen additionally through 
the complete pipeline, eleven of them with both a positive and a negative scenario. 
In the asynchronous transmission mode, the end-to-end login latency did not differ measurably from 
a deployment without interceptors, with an upper bound of 17\,ms for the 95\% confidence interval, 
below five percent of the median login duration. 
Instead, the interceptors cost about 28\,ms of processor time per login, which the IdP spends in 
background threads, whereas blocking transmission added about 60\,ms to each login without saving 
processor time.

The work also identifies the boundaries of the chosen observation point. 
Attack classes located at a different system layer, involving a different party, or observable 
only through their preconditions remain outside its reach, and a provider abusing its own position 
cannot be detected by a system executing inside it. 
Beyond these results, each of the three evaluations uncovered a defect in the infrastructure or the 
tooling of this work that operation itself had not revealed, which illustrates the premise underlying 
the work, namely that a system appearing to function is not thereby known to function.
