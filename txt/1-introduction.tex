\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

Most online services require users to create an account, so that users have to manage a large number 
of credentials and often reuse the same password for several services. 
These accounts are frequently linked to personal data such as an email address, a phone number or 
even an official identity document. 
Attackers exploit password reuse by testing large collections of leaked credentials against other 
services, an attack known as credential stuffing. 
Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report shows that this risk is shifting rather than 
disappearing. 
Credential abuse, the most common initial access vector of the previous year, is down to 13\% of 
more than 22{,}000 confirmed breaches, while the exploitation of vulnerabilities has risen to 
31\%~\cite{VerizonDBIR2026}. 

\Gls{fim} addresses the credential side of this risk by reducing the number of places where 
credentials are created and stored. 
It concentrates authentication behind a single trusted authority, the \gls{idp}, so that users 
can access several \glspl{sp} with one set of credentials. 
When a user wants to access an SP, the SP redirects the user's browser to the IdP. 
The user authenticates with the IdP, which then confirms the user's identity to the 
SP~\cite{FIMSurvey}. 

Shibboleth is one of the most widely deployed open-source implementations of FIM in the research 
and education sector~\cite{Shibboleth}. 
A user authenticates once with the IdP of their organisation, which then issues signed SAML 
assertions that SPs accept as proof of identity. 
The Shibboleth IdP also supports OpenID Connect through an official plugin~\cite{ShibbolethOIDC}. 

Institutions in research and education usually join a national federation such as 
DFN-AAI~\cite{DFNAAI}. 
Its members do not agree on trust bilaterally with every partner. 
Instead, a federation operator registers the IdPs and SPs of all members and publishes their 
metadata, on which every member relies~\cite{DFNMRPS}. 
DFN-AAI and the interfederation service eduGAIN use a full-mesh architecture, in which every 
institution operates its own IdP and exchanges messages directly with the SPs. 
Hub-and-spoke federations route all assertions through a central hub instead, and in their variant 
with central login, a single IdP authenticates the users of all organisations~\cite{IdPSinglePointOfTrust}. 

In a full-mesh federation, the IdP of an institution therefore becomes a single point of trust for 
every SP that its users access. 
Only with central login does one IdP have to be trusted by the entire 
federation~\cite{IdPSinglePointOfTrust}. 
A compromised, misconfigured or vulnerable IdP thus does not affect one application but every SP 
that relies on its assertions. 

The Sunburst supply chain attack shows the consequences of such a compromise~\cite{FireEyeSSC}. 
As Hartl and Derek note, after the initial compromise the attackers obtained the 
trusted certificate used for signing SAML assertions and used it to access all 
services within the targeted enterprise~\cite{FormalSecAnalysisSAML}. 
Since tokens forged with this certificate carry the organisation's own trusted signature, 
the resulting anomalies may go unnoticed~\cite{MicrosoftBlogSSC}. 
Beyond targeted attacks, an IdP is itself software that is reachable from the internet, and SAML 
deployments are exposed to weaknesses in the SAML standard and in its implementations. 
Somorovsky et al. found \gls{xsw} vulnerabilities in eleven of fourteen SAML frameworks, including 
Shibboleth, which allow an attacker to inject forged claims that the SP accepts once the signature 
has been validated~\cite{BreakingSAML}, and the attack has since been reproduced in other 
OpenSAML-based setups~\cite{VulnerSAMLCloud}. 
In a formal analysis of Shibboleth, Sommer identified three further attacks, namely Response 
Injection, Leaked Assertion Artifact and IdP Spoofing, which let an attacker log a victim into an 
account controlled by the attacker or reuse a leaked artifact to log in as the victim. 
The Shibboleth developers attribute the first of these to the SAML standard itself rather than to 
the implementation~\cite{SecAnalysisSSO}.

Despite this elevated risk profile, systematic security monitoring at the application layer of Shibboleth 
IdP deployments remains largely absent. 
Existing approaches such as AAIEye~\cite{MonitoringAAI} focus primarily on operational availability, 
leaving security-relevant aspects unaddressed. 
The research literature reflects a comparable gap. 
A survey of security analyses in FIM finds that Shibboleth has received little attention compared 
with other FIM systems and asks whether this reflects a stronger security posture or simply a lack 
of scrutiny~\cite{FIMSurvey}. 

This thesis addresses this gap by proposing a security-oriented monitoring system that operates 
directly within the Shibboleth application layer. 
It makes application-layer security parameters and authentication workflows visible that tools observing 
the IdP from outside cannot see.
Rather than testing the deployment actively, the system observes it passively. 
This principle has been applied to the eduroam infrastructure by analysing its network 
traffic~\cite{EAPeduroam}, whereas this work applies it inside the application, at the application 
layer of the IdP. 
The system thereby provides the operators of an IdP with a continuous view of its security posture. 

Three research questions guide this work:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} Which security-relevant data can be collected at the application layer of a Shibboleth IdP, and through which mechanisms are these data accessible?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} How can these data be correlated in order to detect configuration weaknesses and anomalies in the authentication process?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} What measurable overhead does the resulting system impose on the monitored IdP?
\end{itemize}

To answer these questions, the system combines three sources whose records can be related to an 
individual login, namely the audit log, two interceptors in the authentication flow and the 
configuration and metadata of the SPs. 
The records are pseudonymised at their source, correlated in a separate backend and evaluated 
against fifteen detection rules. 
The evaluation in a test environment shows that the records of a login can be linked reliably, 
that all rules behave as specified and that the interceptors add no measurable latency to a login. 
The main contributions are an analysis of which security-relevant data are observable from which 
position within the IdP, a monitoring system that correlates these sources across a trust boundary 
and a catalogue of detection rules that includes two rules comparing independently produced records 
of the same login. 

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows. 
Chapter~\ref{ch:background} introduces the technical foundations of FIM, SAML and the Shibboleth IdP. 
Chapter~\ref{ch:related-work} positions this work within existing research on SAML security, application-level 
intrusion detection, federation monitoring and log integrity. 
Chapter~\ref{ch:system-design} derives the requirements and presents the architecture of the 
system, and Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} describes its implementation layer by layer. 
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} evaluates the correlation, the detection rules and the overhead of the system. 
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets the results with respect to the research questions and 
discusses the limitations of the approach, before Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes the thesis.
