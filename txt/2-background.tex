\chapter{Background}\label{ch:background}

This chapter explains the key concepts and terminology relevant to this thesis and establishes 
the technical foundation required for the design of the proposed monitoring tool. 
It is limited to the aspects on which the later chapters build, and consequently covers neither 
the full range of the SAML specification nor the complete configuration surface of the 
Shibboleth IdP.

\section{Technical Foundation of Federated Identity Management}\label{sec:fim}

As introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}, FIM allows users to access resources with a single 
digital identity and provides \gls{sso} functionality across the applications of an organisation. 
The immediate benefit is convenience, since users no longer need to create and remember a 
separate set of credentials for every service. 
The more consequential benefit lies elsewhere. 
Because credentials exist in one place rather than in every participating service, the number of 
systems that store and verify them is reduced, since the SP neither verifies the user's 
authenticators itself nor needs to issue its own~\cite{NIST80063C}. 
The strength of authentication is consequently also determined in one place. 
An SP can require a minimum authentication assurance level, which the IdP fulfils and reports in 
the assertion~\cite{NIST80063C}, and from the second level upwards this requires two distinct 
authentication factors~\cite{NIST80063B}. 
Concentrating authentication at one well-managed site also focuses administrative effort. 
It also makes \gls{mfa} more acceptable to users, since with single sign-on they 
are asked for the second factor once per session at the IdP rather than at every service~\cite{ShibbolethGIAC}. 
Administration follows the same pattern, since accounts are created, modified and revoked 
centrally rather than in each service individually.

This centralisation introduces a new risk in return. 
Every SP that accepts the assertions of an IdP relies on it to authenticate users correctly. 
Concentrating credentials in one place also concentrates the consequences of a compromise, since 
an attacker who gains control of the IdP can potentially authenticate as any of its users at the 
services that rely on it~\cite{IdPSinglePointOfTrust}. 

Both the IdP and the SP therefore have to maintain a high level of security~\cite{NIST80063C}. 
The policies defined for each SP in the IdP must accurately translate to the rules 
at the corresponding SP. 
The configuration of the IdP and that of each SP must remain consistent with one another. 
Certificates, keys and protocol settings are maintained separately on both sides, and a mismatch between 
them can weaken the security of a transaction without either party noticing.
To better understand the interaction within a federated identity system, the roles and responsibilities of 
the IdP and SP are described in the following.

A federation consists of two kinds of participants. 
The IdP authenticates users and issues statements about them~\cite{SAMLOverview}. 
The SP offers the resource a user wants to reach and relies on those statements instead of authenticating 
the user itself. 
Since it relies on the statements of the IdP, the SP is also referred to as the relying party. 
Neither role is meaningful without the other, and their interaction follows a fixed pattern. 

This division has a consequence for how a federation is configured. 
Because the IdP serves many SPs, it does not treat them uniformly. 
It maintains a separate configuration for each partner, specifying for instance whether the statement 
is to be encrypted, which authentication methods are permitted and which attributes are released. 
The SP maintains its own configuration in turn, stating what it expects to receive and what 
it will accept.

Both sides therefore hold configuration that describes the same relationship, and both are 
maintained independently. 
What binds them together is the metadata each party publishes about itself. 
This includes the endpoints at which it can be reached, and the certificates by which its statements can be verified. 
Trust within a federation rests on this metadata being current and correctly exchanged~\cite{SAMLMetadata}. 
Whether the two configurations remain consistent with one another, however, is not something the 
SAML standard itself verifies.

\section{SAML}\label{sec:saml}

SAML is an XML-based standard developed by the \gls{oasis} for the exchange of authentication, 
authorisation and other security-related information between different parties.
SAML is a fundamental component of FIM and is widely used to enable SSO across organisational and 
security boundaries~\cite{SAMLOverview}. 
The current version, SAML 2.0, was published in March 2005 and consolidated earlier approaches, 
among them the Shibboleth architecture~\cite{SAML2Standard}. 

\subsection{SAML Assertions}\label{subsec:assertions}

SAML assertions serve as security tokens that convey security-related information about a principal, 
such as a user, system or service. 
An assertion contains statements made by an issuer about the principal, which may include authentication, 
authorisation and attribute information. 
Since these statements can have security-critical implications, their authenticity and integrity must be 
ensured~\cite{SAMLAssertions}. 
Therefore, the assertion can be protected by a digital signature following the XML Signature standard, 
allowing a relying party to verify that the assertion was issued by the stated issuer and has not been 
modified~\cite{BreakingSAML}. 

Each assertion contains metadata that defines its origin and validity. 
The \texttt{Issuer} element identifies the entity that issued the assertion, while the \texttt{IssueInstant} 
attribute specifies the time at which it was issued. 
Furthermore, every assertion is assigned a unique identifier that must not be reused. 
Once an assertion has been issued, the issuer has no direct control over its subsequent use. 
Consequently, a consumer of an assertion is expected to enforce its specified validity conditions, 
including any validity interval and applicable \texttt{OneTimeUse} condition~\cite{SAMLAssertions}.

\subsection{XML-Signature and Encryption}\label{subsec:xml-sig-enc}

To protect the integrity and authenticity of SAML assertions, XML signatures can be used to 
cryptographically bind the assertion to its issuer. 
The W3C XML Signature standard defines how signatures over XML data are created, encoded and 
verified~\cite{W3CXMLSIG}. 
A signature is defined by the \texttt{Signature} element and can be applied to an entire SAML assertion 
or to selected parts of an XML document~\cite{SAMLAssertions,BreakingSAML}. 

Depending on the location of the signature relative to the signed data, XML signatures can be categorised 
into three types~\cite{W3CXMLSIG}. 
An enveloped signature is contained within the XML element that it signs. 
In contrast, an enveloping signature contains the signed data within the signature itself. 
A detached signature is kept apart from the signed data, which it neither contains nor is contained 
in, and refers to that data by reference~\cite{W3CXMLSIG}. 
In the context of SAML, enveloped signatures are commonly used to sign SAML assertions and protocol 
messages~\cite{SAMLAssertions}.

XML Encryption provides confidentiality for sensitive information contained in SAML messages. 
Instead of transmitting the original XML content in plaintext, the protected content is replaced by 
an \texttt{EncryptedData} element containing the resulting ciphertext~\cite{W3CXMLENC}. 
The encryption therefore enables selective and persistent confidentiality of individual elements within 
an XML document. 
This allows, for example, sensitive attributes contained in a SAML assertion to be encrypted without 
requiring the entire message to be encrypted.

Although XML Signature and XML Encryption provide important security mechanisms, their use and 
configuration depend on the respective SAML profile, deployment and implementation. 
Therefore, SAML is not a security protocol in itself, but rather a framework whose optional features 
can be combined in various ways. 
The resulting security properties depend on the chosen combination. 
Hartl and Derek make this explicit by deriving eight protocol variants from the optional features 
of a single use case and analysing each separately~\cite{FormalSecAnalysisSAML}. 
Consequently, the security properties of a SAML-based system cannot be derived solely from these 
standard mechanisms. 
Incorrect configuration or implementation may result in insufficient protection, such as assertions 
that are not appropriately signed or sensitive information that is transmitted without encryption. 

\subsection{Web Browser SSO Authentication Process}\label{subsec:sso-auth-process}

The SAML Web Browser SSO profile enables a user to authenticate to an IdP and subsequently access a 
protected service provided by an SP. 
The process is mediated by the user's web browser, which forwards SAML messages between the SP and IdP. 

The process begins when a user requests access to a protected resource at the SP. 
If the user does not have an established session, the SP generates a SAML authentication request and 
redirects the user's browser to the IdP. 
The IdP processes the request and authenticates the user using the configured authentication mechanism. 
Upon successful authentication, the IdP generates a SAML response containing a SAML assertion with 
security-related statements about the authenticated principal.

The assertion can be digitally signed by the IdP to provide authenticity and integrity. 
The SAML response is then returned to the user's browser, which forwards it to the SP. 
The SP validates the received SAML response and assertion, including the digital signature and the 
applicable validity conditions. 
If the assertion is considered valid, the SP evaluates the contained statements and attributes to 
determine whether the user is authorised to access the requested resource. 
If the authorisation requirements are satisfied, the SP grants the user access to the protected 
service~\cite{SAMLOverview}.

The use of the user's web browser as an intermediary introduces an important security consideration. 
SAML messages pass through a communication path that is not directly controlled by either the IdP or the SP. 
Therefore, neither party can assume that the messages remain unchanged while being forwarded. 
Consequently, the security of the exchange cannot rely solely on the communication channel. 
Instead, mechanisms such as digital signatures, message identifiers and validity conditions are used 
to ensure the authenticity and integrity of SAML messages and to prevent their misuse~\cite{SAMLSecurityConsideration}.

An important security property of this process is the prevention of message replay. 
SAML requests and assertions contain unique identifiers that allow the SP and IdP to assign messages 
and detect previously processed messages. 
In particular, an assertion's unique identifier must not be reused, and the recipient is expected to 
enforce the applicable validity conditions and replay-related constraints. 

\subsection{SAML Metadata}\label{subsec:saml-metadata}

SAML metadata provides a standardised way for SAML entities to describe their configuration and 
capabilities to other participating entities. 
It enables IdPs and SPs to obtain information required for establishing communication and processing 
SAML messages without requiring every configuration parameter to be exchanged manually. 
The SAML metadata specification organises this information around roles supported by an entity, 
which are represented using role descriptors within an \texttt{EntityDescriptor} element~\cite{SAMLMetadata}.

For an IdP, the \texttt{IDPSSODescriptor} describes information relevant to its role as a SAML IdP. 
Similarly, an SP can be described using an \texttt{SPSSODescriptor}. 
These descriptors contain information such as supported SAML protocols, communication endpoints, 
supported bindings and cryptographic keys. 
For example, an SP metadata document can specify its \gls{acs} endpoint and the bindings supported 
for receiving SAML responses, while an IdP can specify its SSO Service endpoints and the corresponding 
supported bindings. 
A binding defines how a SAML message is transported over an underlying protocol, for instance in the 
query string of an HTTP redirect (HTTP-Redirect) or in an HTML form that the browser submits 
(HTTP-POST)~\cite{SAMLBinding}. 

Cryptographic information contained in metadata is particularly important for the security of SAML 
communication. 
Metadata can contain certificates and public keys used by participating entities for verifying digital 
signatures or encrypting SAML messages. 
Consequently, the metadata configuration directly influences how SAML messages are validated and which 
endpoints and cryptographic keys are considered valid by an implementation.

The security of the metadata itself is therefore an important aspect of a SAML deployment. 
If an entity accepts manipulated metadata, an attacker could potentially influence endpoints, 
supported bindings or cryptographic keys used during SAML message processing. 
SAML metadata can itself be protected using digital signatures, allowing an entity to verify the integrity 
and origin of the metadata before using it for configuration or trust decisions~\cite{SAMLMetadata}.

\section{Shibboleth}\label{sec:shibboleth}

Shibboleth is a free and open-source web single sign-on software suite that 
implements SAML and other standards for FIM. 
The Shibboleth Consortium provides separate components for the IdP and the SP~\cite{Shibboleth}. 
This thesis concerns the IdP, which is implemented as a Java-based web application and is typically 
deployed using a Java servlet container such as Apache Tomcat. 
User identities and associated attributes are commonly managed through an external 
directory service, such as a \gls{ldap} server, which provides the IdP with the 
information required for authentication and attribute release. 
The IdP builds on the OpenSAML library for the construction and validation of SAML messages, which is relevant 
because vulnerabilities reported for OpenSAML can affect Shibboleth deployments as well~\cite{ShibbolethGIAC}.

\subsection{Profile Flows and Interception Points}\label{subsec:profile-flows}

The IdP processes each request as a workflow built on Spring Web Flow, a framework in which a 
sequence of processing steps is described declaratively rather than in program code. 
A flow consists of states that are traversed in order, and a state may delegate to a subflow, 
that is a self-contained flow which performs a defined task and returns control to its caller. 
Every top-level flow of the IdP implements one of the supported protocol interfaces, referred to 
as profiles, such as SAML 2.0 Web Browser \gls{sso}.
Recurring tasks within such a profile, among them the authentication of the user, are carried out 
by subflows~\cite{ProfileHandling}. 

To allow developers to extend this behaviour without copying or editing the internal flow definitions, 
the IdP provides a set of defined hooks. 
A subflow attached to one of these hooks is known as an interceptor, and there are three hooks in total: 
inbound message processing, post authentication processing and outbound message processing. 
The first and third are available in any profile flow, whereas the second applies only to profiles that 
actually authenticate a user~\cite{ProfileHandling}.
An interceptor is registered as a bean and enabled by adding its flow identifier to the corresponding list 
property of a profile configuration in \texttt{conf/relying-party.xml}. 
No modification of the IdP source code is required~\cite{ProfileInterceptConfiguration}.

\subsection{The Context Tree}\label{subsec:context-tree}

While a flow describes the sequence of processing steps, the state of the request being processed 
is held separately in a structure referred to as the context tree. 
Its root is the \texttt{ProfileRequestContext}, which is created when a request arrives and 
accompanies it through the entire profile execution. 
Attached to this root are child contexts, each holding one aspect of the request, so that the 
state grows as processing advances rather than being assembled in a single object at the 
outset~\cite{GeneralArchitecture, ProfileRequestContext}. 

Three of these child contexts are relevant in the following. 
The inbound \texttt{MessageContext} holds the message as it was received, the 
\texttt{RelyingPartyContext} identifies the SP the request originates from and records whether 
that identity could be verified, and the \texttt{SubjectContext} holds the authenticated subject 
once authentication has completed~\cite{ProfileHandling}. 
The last of these illustrates the incremental nature of the structure, since it does not exist 
before the user has been authenticated.

\subsection{Audit Logging}\label{subsec:audit-logging}

The IdP writes one line per completed transaction to \texttt{idp-audit.log}. 
The set of fields is not fixed but defined by the operator in \texttt{conf/audit.xml}, so the 
information available in the log depends on the configuration of the individual 
deployment. 
In the default configuration, authentication attempts that do not result in a completed transaction, 
in particular failed password validations, produce no entry in this log. 
The IdP can additionally record every authentication attempt, but this feature is disabled by default, 
and when enabled its records enter the same audit stream unless they are routed to a separate 
destination~\cite{AuditLoggingConfiguration}. 

The format of a log line is defined per profile in the \textit{shibboleth.AuditFormattingMap} of 
\texttt{conf/audit.xml}. 
A format consists of formatting tokens, each of which is replaced by a value from the context tree 
of the transaction when the line is written, for instance the time of the event, the identifier of 
the inbound message or the address of the client. 
Some values are written in an abbreviated form, which the IdP derives from a replacement map, 
and further tokens can be added by the operator~\cite{AuditLoggingConfiguration}.

\subsection{Configuration}\label{subsec:config}

Three configuration files are relevant in the following. 
\texttt{relying-party.xml} holds the per-partner configuration described in Section~\ref{sec:fim}, 
including which profiles are enabled and which security settings apply~\cite{RelyingPartyConfiguration}. 
\texttt{metadata-providers.xml} declares where the \acrshort{idp} obtains metadata for 
its partners~\cite{MetadataConfiguration}. 
\texttt{attribute-filter.xml} governs which attributes are released to which 
relying party~\cite{AttributeFilterConfiguration}.

The settings of \texttt{relying-party.xml} apply to all partners by default and can be overridden 
for a single partner, so that one partner is configured differently from the others. 
They include the authentication flows permitted for a partner and whether responses and assertions 
are signed or encrypted~\cite{RelyingPartyConfiguration}.
