\chapter{Related Work}\label{ch:related-work}

Existing research and implementations were examined to identify approaches relevant to the 
development of a monitoring system for an IdP operating at the application layer. 
The primary objective was to identify solutions that address the overarching goal of this 
thesis and to analyse their respective limitations and areas of applicability.

These related works provide valuable insights and a foundation for this thesis by identifying 
existing approaches, their areas of coverage, and gaps that remain relevant to the 
considered scenario. 
The most relevant studies and projects are presented and analysed in the following sections.

\section{Why Monitor from the Inside?}\label{sec:why-inside}

As discussed in the introduction~\ref{ch:introduction}, the proposed monitoring system operates 
directly within the application layer of the Shibboleth IdP. 
Its objective is to detect attacks based on events and behaviour observable from 
within the application.

The underlying concept is not new. 
The OWASP AppSensor project describes an approach in which intrusion detection and response 
capabilities are integrated directly into an application~\cite{OWASPAppSensor}. 
In contrast to network-based security mechanisms, application-layer monitoring can make use of 
information about the application's internal state and semantics.

A network firewall primarily controls network communication, while a network-based \acrfull{ids} 
has limited visibility into application-specific behaviour. 
A \acrfull{waf} can provide additional protection against generic application-layer attacks, such 
as SQL injection, but typically has limited knowledge of the internal logic and state of a specific 
application. 
Consequently, application-integrated detection mechanisms can complement network- and perimeter-based 
security controls by analysing application-specific events and behaviour~\cite{OWASPAppSensor}.

In the following, the proposed system is classified using the taxonomy for self-protecting software 
systems introduced by Yuan and Malek~\cite{Taxonomy}. 
Two dimensions of this taxonomy are particularly relevant to the scope of this work: the 
\texttt{Architecture Level} and the \texttt{Self-Protection Level}.

The \texttt{Architecture Level} describes the layer at which a security mechanism operates and 
distinguishes between Network, Host, Software/Application, and Services. 
This dimension allows the observation position of different security mechanisms to be compared. 
Network-based \acrfull{ids}, such as Suricata and Snort, operate primarily at the network level, 
whereas \acrfull{edr} solutions operate at the host level. 
The proposed system operates at the Software / Application level, as it observes events directly 
within the Shibboleth IdP. 
The distinction between these systems therefore lies primarily in the layer at which 
security-relevant information becomes observable.

The second relevant dimension is the \texttt{Self-Protection Level}, which describes the degree of 
self-protection according to the MAPE process of Monitor, Analyze, Plan, and Execute. 
The proposed system fully covers the \textit{Monitor & Detect} level by collecting and detecting 
security-relevant events within the IdP. 
The \textit{Analyze & Characterize} level is partially addressed through event correlation and 
severity classification. 
In contrast, \textit{Plan & Prevent} is outside the scope of this work. 
The system generates alerts based on detected events but does not autonomously modify the system 
state or take preventive countermeasures.

The resulting classification is a direct consequence of the underlying design principle of 
this work. 
The proposed system follows the concept of a \textit{Looking Glass}, in which security-relevant 
events are made observable without interfering with the operation of the monitored system. 
Rieckers applies a similar principle to the passive observation of a 
federation infrastructure~\cite{EAPeduroam}, which is adopted by this work. 
Extending the system with automatic response mechanisms would contradict this passive 
observation principle. 
Furthermore, erroneous automated responses could negatively affect the availability or operation 
of the IdP and thereby potentially impact multiple SPs that rely on it.

Two precedents:
- BlackWatch:
    - same architekture for generic web application, asynchonous transfer identical reasoning
    - The main challenge here is one aspect: 
        - Developers must integrate an identification code into their own application. 
        - This work uses a designated extension point
    
\section{Why SIEM Functionalities is not Enough}\label{sec:why-not-siem}

The limitation stems from the definition
- A SIEM processes logs and events. 
- This leads to two limitations: Anything not recorded in a log is invisible. 
- Anything recorded in a log but that has been tampered with is considered valid

The closest equivalent: Wazuh
- Combines log analysis, file integrity monitoring, and configuration assessment
- The limitation lies in how runtime events are handled: 
    - Configuration is checked in isolation against static policies, not linked to a specific login
- Example: MFA-BYPASS—A login without MFA goes unnoticed. 
- A login without MFA on a service provider that requires MFA is flagged as an anomaly

Log Integrity as a Structural Difference:
- Graves documents that log entries in Shibboleth can be manipulated
- OWASP A09 lists the lack of protection against manipulation as a separate vulnerability, citing CWE-117
- A system that solely consumes logs can compare multiple log sources, but all are files on the same system and are exposed to the same class of attackers
- Interceptor reports and audit entries are generated independently and may contradict each other

Distinction from the Cryptographic Alternative:
- Schneier, Kelsey, and Snodgrass make manipulation cryptographically detectable, at the cost of restructuring the logging layer and relying on external trust services. 
    - Snodgrass reports up to 16 percent overhead
- This approach does not provide cryptographic proof, but it does not require any intervention in the logging chain
- To be honest: The evaluation of discrepancies as a security signal has not yet been implemented. 
    - The mechanism already detects them, but evaluates them as a quality metric.

The Gap in AppSensor:
- All detection points monitor a user within a session
- Three rules have equivalents there: AE2, AE3, and AE13/SE5/SE6
- What the schema does not account for is a target-vs.-actual comparison. 
    - AppSensor was designed for applications that authenticate their own users. 
    - An IdP authenticates on behalf of third parties, and part of the security-relevant states lies in the configuration of its partners
- A Detection Point, on the other hand, is compatible with: IE6, Violation of Security Log Integrity

\section{Derivation of the Rules}\label{sec:rules}

Three Reference Frameworks:
- FIM Survey by Simpson and Groß: eleven vulnerabilities, fourteen attack classes, linked in cross-protocol issues. 
    - Six concern SAML, one concerns Shibboleth
- OASIS Security Considerations: prescriptive guidelines on what a deployment should do
- STRIDE according to Graves: what kind of impact an attack would have

Varying levels of evidence: 
- The survey empirically demonstrates that an attack occurred. 
- OASIS specifies what measures are intended to counter it. 
- Where both are present, as with the replay rules, the reasoning is strongest.

Two observations from the table
- “Lack of Binding” appears in three of the six SAML rows, making it the most frequently documented SAML vulnerability. 
    - The replay rules address precisely this
- Several rules form “target-actual” pairs: the same vulnerability, once as a configuration state and once as an event that has occurred. 
    - This is a standalone argument in favor of the three-source architecture
- It should be noted that two rules cannot be assigned to any grid. 
    - SUSPICIOUS-USER-AGENT is an indicator with no documented link to an attack, while BEHAVIOUR-ANOMALY is a detection method rather than an attack class.

Severity:
- Derived from Graves risk scale R1 through R5
- Two-tiered, because the rules detect partly enabling factors and partly events
- Two rules were downgraded and adjusted in the code

\section{What Is Structurally Impossible}\label{sec:structureally-impossible}

It demonstrates that the limitations of the approach have been thoroughly considered rather than merely listed.

Three Groups of Reasons:
- Other System Levels: 
    - Weak DNS and DNS poisoning affect name resolution. 
    - The same applies to DDoS and UI redressing
- Other party: 
    - Vulnerable SP and CSRF occur between the browser and the SP. 
    - The same applies to XSS and session swapping
- Only indirectly observable: 
    - Lack of Binding and MITM. 
    - The prerequisites are observable, not the event itself

Two special cases should be highlighted:
- No Trust Infrastructure and Bogus Merchant is the only Shibboleth-related entry in the survey and remains open. 
    - The malicious SP is registered normally and is formally legitimate.
- A malicious provider is structurally undetectable. 
    - Since the system runs within the IdP, the observer and the potential attacker are one and the same. 
    - This limitation applies to every IdP-side system.

And now, the connection to motivation: 
- None of the thirteen rules reaches R5.
- The Sunburst case from the beginning, specifically, would not be detectable with this system.
