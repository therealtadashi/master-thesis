\chapter{Discussion}\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter interprets the results with respect to the research questions, discusses 
the structural limitations of the approach and outlines directions for future work. 

\section{Interpretation of the Results}\label{sec:interpretation}

The results of this study can be read along the three research questions posed in 
Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}. 
The first question asked which security relevant data can be collected at the application layer 
of a Shibboleth IdP and through which mechanisms. 
Three sources proved usable, namely the audit log, the configuration and metadata of the SPs, 
and the runtime state accessible through interceptors. 
The value of this combination does not primarily result from the number of sources but from the 
positions at which they observe the authentication process. 
The audit log records the outcome of a completed transaction, the configuration states what was 
intended for the partner involved, and the interceptors observe the incoming request and the 
outgoing response while the transaction is being processed. 
Each source therefore holds information that the others cannot provide, and the complementarity 
follows from their position in the processing sequence rather than from a deliberate division of 
responsibilities. 

The second question asked how these data can be correlated in order to detect configuration 
weaknesses and anomalies. 
The correlation found a counterpart for every report, and with the inbound message identifier or at 
least one hash that separates the users, it found the correct one in every tested scenario. 
Only the strategy that relies on time alone (\texttt{TIME\_\allowbreak{}WINDOW}) assigned reports to 
the wrong entry, and only where logins at the same SP followed each other within less than 100\,ms. 
The hashed heuristic (\texttt{HASH\_\allowbreak{}AND\_\allowbreak{}TIME\_\allowbreak{}WINDOW}) remains 
reliable up to about three logins per second for a given combination of subject, address and SP, a rate 
that interactive users do not reach (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-correlation-results}). 
The fifteen detection rules behave as intended for the scenarios constructed for them. 
All of them are verified against a real Elasticsearch instance, thirteen are also tested through the 
complete pipeline, eleven of them with a detected condition. 
Whether they also detect real attacks reliably and without false alarms in production cannot be 
derived from these tests, since this would require productive logins labelled independently of the 
rules (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-detection-results}).

The number of alerts deserves attention as well. 
During the performance series, the detection layer raised 21{,}712 alerts for 10{,}854 logins, 
since the benchmark used a script client against the weak SP and both affected rules report every 
login (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-detection-results}). 
In a production environment, such a high volume of alerts would make it difficult to identify the few 
that indicate an attack, potentially leading to alert fatigue. 
The two rules rated low report indicators that are of limited value on their own. 
In the evaluation, they served to verify the pipeline end to end, but in production they mainly add 
volume. 
\texttt{FAILED\_\allowbreak{}LOGIN} reports every failed login attempt, although 
\texttt{BRUTE\_\allowbreak{}FORCE} evaluates the same attempts and reports a subject once they 
accumulate. 
\texttt{SUSPICIOUS\_\allowbreak{}USER\_\allowbreak{}AGENT} alone accounts for half of the alerts of 
the performance series. 
In production, \texttt{FAILED\_\allowbreak{}LOGIN} could therefore be hidden without affecting the 
detection of brute force attacks, and \texttt{SUSPICIOUS\_\allowbreak{}USER\_\allowbreak{}AGENT} 
could be shown in condensed form, for instance as a daily count per SP. 

The third question asked what overhead the resulting system imposes. 
With asynchronous transmission, the latency of a login did not differ measurably from a deployment 
without interceptors. 
The point estimate is $+6.2$\,ms, and the upper bound of the 95\,\% confidence interval is 17\,ms, 
which is below five percent of the median login duration. 
The work inside the two interceptors amounts to about 0.45\,ms. 
The cost appears as processor time instead, about 28\,ms per login or 22 percent of the processor 
time of a login without interceptors, which the IdP spends in background threads. 
Synchronous transmission adds about 60\,ms to every login and was the slower configuration in all 
18 runs, without saving processor time. 
In the current implementation it would not improve the delivery either, since both modes only log 
a failed transmission (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-overhead-results}). 
The measurement therefore supports the choice of fire-and-forget transmission. 

Beyond the individual research questions, each of the three evaluations uncovered at least one 
defect, six in total (Table~\ref{tab:defects}). 
None of them caused a visible failure. 
They became apparent only through a comparison with an expectation defined in advance, such as the 
ground truth of the correlation or an end-to-end test against the clean SP. 
Tests at several levels were therefore necessary to establish that the system works as intended. 

\section{Limitations of the Approach}\label{sec:limitation}

This section discusses the structural limitations of the proposed monitoring system. 
As the assignment in Table~\ref{tab:rules} shows, the rules address two of the six SAML 
cross-protocol issues identified by Simpson and Groß~\cite{FIMSurvey}, namely unencrypted 
communications exploited by message modification and lack of binding exploited by replay. 
The second is covered only in part, since a replayed assertion is presented to the SP and does not 
pass the IdP again, so that only the replay of a request remains observable (Table~\ref{tab:detection-coverage}).

This limited coverage is not primarily caused by missing implementations but by the observation 
position of the monitoring system. 
The four remaining issues lie at a different system layer, at a different party or outside the path 
that the IdP observes~\cite{FIMSurvey}. 
DNS poisoning concerns name resolution and therefore a layer beneath the application~\cite{FIMSurvey}. 
The IdP processes the request it receives regardless of how the client resolved the corresponding 
address. 
Cross-site request forgery describes an implementation vulnerability at the SP~\cite{FIMSurvey}. 
The relevant interaction occurs between the browser and the SP and therefore does not pass 
through the IdP. 
The same applies to message modification enabled by a lack of binding, whose mitigation lies in 
the SP checking the credentials it receives~\cite{FIMSurvey}. 
A man-in-the-middle attack exploits a lack of binding, where a token is not tied to the SP for which 
it was issued, and the survey names binding the token to its intended SP as the countermeasure~\cite{FIMSurvey}. 
The attack itself takes place on the communication path after the message has left the IdP. 
The IdP can at most observe whether an outgoing assertion is restricted to its intended recipient. 
None of the implemented rules currently examines this condition. 

Other attack classes of the survey, such as distributed denial of service, cross-site scripting 
and session swapping, are not reported for SAML~\cite{FIMSurvey}. 
Two further attack classes, UI redressing and ACS spoofing, were reported only for SAML and exploit a 
vulnerable IdP~\cite{FIMSurvey}. 
UI redressing is mitigated by response headers that the web server in front of the IdP sets~\cite{GuardiansClouds, ShibbolethGIAC}. 
These headers lie outside the profile flows that the interceptors observe. 
ACS spoofing, in contrast, could be detected by comparing the requested endpoint with the endpoints in 
the metadata of the SP, but the current catalogue contains no such rule~\cite{GuardiansClouds, MetadataForSP}. 

Two cases deserve separate attention. 
The bogus merchant scenario is the only cross-protocol issue that concerns Shibboleth directly, and 
it remains uncovered by the monitoring system because a malicious SP is a regular participant registered 
in the federation metadata. 
From the perspective of the IdP, such an SP does not differ from any other partner. 
Monitoring changes to its configuration and metadata would only reveal a provider whose behaviour has 
changed, not one that was malicious from the start. 

The second case is more fundamental. 
The malicious provider scenario is reported for other protocols, among them Liberty, which builds 
on SAML~\cite{FIMSurvey}. 
It describes an IdP that abuses its own position within the federation and therefore concerns 
any IdP, regardless of the protocol. 
Since the monitoring system is integrated into the IdP, the observer and the potential attacker 
reside within the same system. 
An entity that fully controls the IdP therefore also controls the collection layer of the 
monitoring system, including which records are produced and which are not. 
This limitation follows directly from the observation point and cannot be overcome by adding 
further detection rules within the same trust domain. 
Snodgrass et al. make the same assumption in their threat model, where only services outside the 
compromised system remain trustworthy once an adversary has gained control of 
it~\cite{TamperDetection}. 
Detecting this class of behaviour would require an observation point outside the IdP, for instance 
at the SPs or at the level of the federation itself. 

A final limitation connects back to the motivation of this work. 
The risk classification used for the severity assignment in Section~\ref{subsec:severity} 
distinguishes five levels, of which the highest describes an adversary able to authenticate as any 
user at any SP. 
None of the fifteen rules reaches this level, since it presupposes a compromise of the signing key. 
An adversary in possession of that key produces formally valid assertions that are 
indistinguishable from legitimate ones by any of the implemented rules. 
The Sunburst incident described in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} is precisely such a case and 
would therefore not have been detected by this system. 
What the system can detect are configuration weaknesses that make an attack of this kind more 
likely to succeed or harder to notice, which is a narrower claim than the motivation might 
initially suggest.

\section{Implications for Future Research}\label{sec:implication}

The findings and limitations identified in this study point to several directions for future work 
in the area of application layer monitoring for federated identity infrastructures. 
They fall into two categories, namely extensions that would widen the observation point and 
questions that cannot be answered without a different kind of data.

A primary direction is to extend the detection capabilities beyond the IdP and incorporate 
observations made at the SP. 
The approach presented here is inherently limited by its position, as discussed in 
Section~\ref{sec:limitation}, and several of the uncovered attack classes are located precisely 
where the IdP has no visibility. 
Martín et al. have proposed behavioural anomaly detection on the relying party 
side~\cite{UEBAFederation}, arguing that delegating authentication does not transfer the entire 
security responsibility. 
Combining both perspectives would address opposite ends of the same federation. 
Such an extension would require a suitable and preferably standardised integration mechanism for 
SPs, which introduces organisational challenges beyond the technical ones, since it 
presupposes cooperation between parties that are administratively independent.

A second direction concerns the observation point within the IdP itself. 
The outbound interceptor added in this work makes the response level fields available from a second, 
independent source, and it enables the comparison with the audit entry described in 
Section~\ref{subsec:detection}. 
What it cannot provide are the assertion internal fields of encrypted assertions, since the 
encryption has already taken place at that hook. 
An observation point before the encryption would close this gap, but the interception points that 
Shibboleth offers to developers do not include one, and an intervention in the profile flow would 
conflict with the third requirement from Section~\ref{sec:requirements}. 
Whether the audit extraction points of the IdP could serve this purpose is a question for future work.

A third direction addresses the one Shibboleth specific issue that remains uncovered. 
The system already receives every changed configuration and metadata document of an SP 
(Section~\ref{sec:impl-aggregation}), but no rule reports the change itself. 
Such a rule would provide a partial answer to the bogus merchant scenario, since a change is the 
only observable signal where no basis for prior trust exists. 
What such a mechanism could establish and what it could not is discussed in 
Section~\ref{sec:limitation}, and the distinction would need to be stated carefully in any 
implementation.

A fourth direction is the extension of the approach to \gls{oidc}~\cite{OpenIDConnectCore}. 
The present work focuses exclusively on SAML and Shibboleth, so that it remains an open question 
whether the three source architecture developed here transfers to OIDC based infrastructures. 
The architecture itself appears protocol independent, since an OIDC provider likewise maintains a 
registration of its clients, writes records of completed transactions and processes incoming 
requests at runtime. 
The detection rules, by contrast, are protocol specific and would not transfer, since conditions 
such as a missing proof key exchange or an implicit flow have no direct counterpart in the rules 
developed here~\cite{RFC7636, RFC9700}. 
Future work could therefore examine which application level sources exist in an OIDC provider, 
whether they are complementary in the same sense and which rules follow from the documented 
weaknesses of that protocol. 

Finally, one question cannot be addressed by extending the system at all. 
Determining how reliably the rules detect genuine attacks requires productive traffic in which 
each authentication is independently labelled, as discussed in 
Section~\ref{subsec:eval-detection-results}. 
Producing such a dataset is a research problem in its own right rather than an implementation 
task, and it would benefit not only this work but any evaluation of detection systems in federated 
identity infrastructures, where the same constraint applies.
